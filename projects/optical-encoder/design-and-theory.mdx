## The Challenge: Precision Rotary Position Data on a Budget

The objective of this project was to develop a cost-effective, high-resolution alternative to commercially available optical encoders. While commercial units offer high precision, their cost can be a significant barrier for hobbyists and rapid prototyping. By leveraging 3D printing for mechanical components and a custom AVR-based firmware, it's possible to create a highly capable sensor for a fraction of the price.

---

## Core Principle: Sensing Direction with Quadrature Encoding

The fundamental challenge for any rotary encoder is not just counting pulses, but also determining the direction of rotation. This is solved using a technique called **quadrature encoding**.

Instead of one sensor, two infrared (IR) sensors, typically labeled **Channel A** and **Channel B**, are positioned over the encoder disk's tracks. They are intentionally offset so that as the disk spins, the digital pulses they generate are **90 degrees out of phase**.

The microcontroller's firmware constantly monitors the state of both channels. By observing which channel's signal rises first, it can instantly and reliably determine the direction of rotation:

- If **Channel A leads Channel B**, the shaft is spinning clockwise.
- If **Channel B leads Channel A**, the shaft is spinning counter-clockwise.

## System Architecture: From Hardware to GUI

The entire system is designed as a modular data acquisition pipeline, moving information from the physical world to a digital interface for analysis.

[Image of a system block diagram]

The data flows through four distinct stages:

1.  **Encoder Hardware:** A 3D printed disk with 72 slots spins between two IR sensors. The physical rotation is translated into raw digital square waves on Channel A and Channel B.

2.  **AVR Microcontroller:** This is the brain of the operation. It uses hardware interrupts tied to the sensor pins. This is critical, as an interrupt-driven design ensures that **no pulse is ever missed**, even at high rotational speeds.

3.  **Data Transmission:** Upon each interrupt (a change in rotation), the firmware captures the new shaft position and a high-resolution timestamp. This data packet is then serialized and transmitted to a computer via USART.

4.  **PC-Side Processing:** A custom GUI application running on a computer receives the serial data via a USB connection. It deserializes the packets and uses the position and time data to plot real-time charts, calculate velocity, and log the information for further analysis.

## Key Design Decisions

Several key engineering decisions were made to meet the project's goals:

- **Low Cost:** Using 3D printing for the encoder wheel and enclosure dramatically reduces the bill of materials compared to using machined metal or glass disks.
- **High Resolution:** The encoder disk was designed with 72 slots, which, when combined with quadrature encoding (reading all four edge transitions of the A and B signals), results in **288 distinct positions** per revolution.
- **Real-Time Data:** The interrupt-driven firmware provides near-instantaneous feedback. Data is transmitted not only on a fixed timer but also _the moment_ a change in position is detected.
- **Rich Data Output:** Instead of just sending a position count, the system also sends a `delta T` (timestamp) with each interrupt. This allows the PC-side software to accurately calculate not just position, but also angular velocity and acceleration.
